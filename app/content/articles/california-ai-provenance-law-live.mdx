California's AI Transparency Act became operative on August 2, closing a two-year gap between the law's signing and its enforcement. Under the statute, generative AI providers with more than one million monthly users in California must now offer a free, publicly accessible tool that can detect whether content came from their own systems, and must embed provenance information directly into the images, video, and audio they generate.

That provenance takes two forms. A manifest disclosure is the visible label a viewer can see and recognize on sight. A latent disclosure is metadata baked into the file itself — provider, system version, and timestamp — designed to survive editing and be difficult to strip out. Removing that metadata, or building a tool whose purpose is to remove it, is now explicitly prohibited.

## Why the delay was deliberate

The law's original effective date was January 1, 2026. Assembly Bill 853, signed in October 2025, pushed it back seven months on purpose. Lawmakers wanted California's watermarking rules to land on the same day the European Union begins enforcing Article 50 of the AI Act, its own provenance mandate for high-risk systems. The result is the first serious attempt at transatlantic alignment on AI content labeling, even though the two frameworks were written independently and don't map onto each other perfectly.

AB 853 also widened who has to comply. The original bill targeted the companies building generative AI systems. The amendment adds obligations for the large platforms that host and distribute that content, and for camera and device manufacturers, who must now give users the option to embed a latent disclosure by default when they capture real photos or video — a provision aimed less at AI companies and more at making authentic content verifiably authentic.

## The federal half of the story is still blank

California's rules are now live and carry civil penalties of five thousand dollars per day, per violation. The federal government's equivalent step is not. Executive Order 14409 set an August 1 deadline for a classified benchmarking process, a voluntary frontier-model disclosure framework, and a federal cyber-workforce plan — and missed it, with no Federal Register notice, NIST publication, or OSTP statement to explain the delay. Frontier labs building products for both markets are left holding a working definition from Sacramento and no equivalent definition from Washington for what counts as a "covered" model at all.
